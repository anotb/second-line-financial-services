# cross-cutting

One section per topic. Read only the sections the scope calls for.

## climate

### Cross-cutting overlay — climate

Loaded when the scope flags climate. Implementation plans triggered by or touching climate-risk rules carry distinct data, governance, and disclosure patterns.

#### Large-bank climate-risk principles (Interagency)

For banks $100B+ in assets, the OCC, FRB, and FDIC's Principles for Climate-Related Financial Risk Management (October 2023) frame climate-risk management. Implementation plans integrate with the bank's enterprise risk management framework. Workstream elements typically include:

- governance (board and management oversight);
- policies, procedures, and limits;
- strategic planning;
- risk management (identification, measurement, monitoring of physical and transition risks);
- data, risk measurement, and reporting;
- scenario analysis;
- third-party / vendor inclusion of climate risk.

The principles are not a rulemaking and do not carry a fixed compliance date; the plan's effective date follows the bank's internal target and any supervisory letter expectations.

#### SEC climate disclosure rule

The SEC's Climate-Related Disclosures rule (March 2024 final, with subsequent litigation pause) brings disclosure requirements for public companies. Implementation plans triggered by the rule (where it is operative) cover Regulation S-K Item 1500 series and Regulation S-X Article 14 disclosures, including governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics (Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions for accelerated and large accelerated filers, with phased dates).

The plan's status reflects the rule's litigation posture; where the rule is paused, the plan continues at a slower cadence as a contingency exercise. Voluntary frameworks (ISSB IFRS S2, TCFD, CDP) often run in parallel.

#### State-level climate disclosure (California)

California SB 253 (Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act) and SB 261 (Climate-Related Financial Risk Act) impose climate-disclosure requirements on companies doing business in California above specified revenue thresholds. The plan's scope captures California operations and the entity-level revenue thresholds; California Air Resources Board (CARB) implementation regulations frame the technical requirements.

#### NAIC climate-risk disclosure for insurers

For insurers, the NAIC Climate Risk Disclosure Survey (TCFD-aligned) is the framework adopted by lead-state DOIs. Implementation plans for an insurer's first or refreshed climate-risk disclosure integrate with the insurer's ORSA process (the climate-risk lens within ORSA) and the lead state's disclosure cycle.

#### Workstream shape

Typical climate workstream pattern:

- governance updates (board oversight charter, named C-level role, committee inclusion);
- policy and procedure updates (climate risk in credit, market, operational, reputational risk frameworks);
- data sourcing for emissions, physical risk, and transition risk;
- scenario analysis build;
- third-party and vendor climate-risk inclusion in TPRM;
- disclosure narrative drafting where rules require disclosure;
- audit committee / board reporting cadence.

#### Data-readiness as the binding constraint

Climate plans frequently encounter data-readiness as the binding constraint: emissions data quality, supply-chain Scope 3 visibility, climate-scenario data availability. The plan's risk register highlights data-readiness as a top risk, with explicit milestones for vendor data acquisition and internal data-quality remediation.

#### Evidence patterns

Climate acceptance criteria emphasise documented governance (board minutes, committee charters, named oversight roles), documented scenario-analysis results with methodology, and where rules require disclosure, the actual disclosure document with iXBRL tagging or equivalent.

#### Sources

Named regulatory anchors for climate implementation are in `references/source-anchors.md`. Citations include the OCC/FRB/FDIC Principles for Climate-Related Financial Risk Management (October 2023), SEC Climate-Related Disclosures rule (Release Nos. 33-11275; 34-99678, March 2024), California SB 253 and SB 261, NAIC Climate Risk Disclosure Survey, and voluntary frameworks (ISSB IFRS S2, TCFD).

## conduct

### Cross-cutting overlay — conduct

Loaded when the scope flags conduct. Implementation plans triggered by or touching unfair-deceptive-or-abusive-acts-or-practices (UDAAP), fair-lending, fair-treatment, or consumer-redress findings carry distinct customer-impact and remediation patterns.

#### UDAAP remediation under CFPB and FTC

Implementation plans triggered by a UDAAP finding (CFPB, FTC, OCC, FRB, FDIC, NCUA) cover three dimensions:

- the act or practice itself (cease the conduct, change the disclosure, change the product feature);
- the affected-population identification and consumer redress;
- the prospective controls that prevent recurrence.

For consent-order-driven UDAAP remediation, the order generally specifies the remediation framework, the redress methodology, the monitor or independent consultant role, and the reporting cadence. The implementation plan inherits the order's structure.

#### Consumer-redress design

Redress milestones cover identifying the affected population (often the hardest analytical step), calculating the redress amount per consumer, designing the disbursement mechanism (account credit, check, electronic payment), drafting consumer communications, and reporting the disbursement to the regulator. The plan names the redress methodology and the reconciliation process.

For disbursement to consumers no longer at the firm, the plan addresses last-known-address handling, escheatment, and the regulator-acceptable retry process. For disbursement above tax-reporting thresholds, the plan addresses 1099 reporting.

#### Fair-lending remediation

Implementation plans for fair-lending findings (ECOA, Reg B, FHA, HMDA, state fair-lending laws) carry pricing, underwriting, marketing, and steering dimensions. Remediation generally includes:

- statistical-analysis update on the affected loan population;
- consumer redress where pricing or denial harms are quantified;
- model and overlay review (where credit decisioning is implicated);
- training across in-scope underwriters and originators;
- ongoing monitoring with HMDA, ECOA, and state-specific testing programmes;
- second-line independent fair-lending review.

The CFPB ECOA examination procedures and the OCC, FRB, FDIC fair-lending examination procedures frame the supervisory expectations. For mortgage-side fair-lending, HMDA reporting under Regulation C is the data source.

#### Sales-practices and conduct overlays

For sales-practices remediation (overdraft, deposit-account fee structures, add-on products, telesales, account-opening incentive plans), the plan covers:

- product-design changes;
- compensation and incentive review;
- sales-process control updates;
- complaint-handling integration;
- consumer redress;
- monitoring through complaint analytics, mystery shopping, and call-recording sampling.

#### Complaint-handling

Implementation plans triggered by complaint-handling findings name the policy, the complaint-categorisation taxonomy, the SLAs, the root-cause-analysis process, and the trending and reporting cadence to senior management and the board. CFPB and OCC examiner expectations on complaint-handling frame the design; published consent orders inform the structural pattern.

#### Workstream shape

Typical conduct workstream pattern:

- act-or-practice cessation (often immediate, ahead of the rest of the plan);
- affected-population identification (data-heavy);
- redress design and disbursement;
- consumer communications;
- prospective control build;
- training;
- monitoring programme update;
- regulator reporting cadence.

#### Governance shape

Conduct-driven plans typically run with named board oversight given the customer-impact dimension. For consent-order remediation, monthly or quarterly board reporting is the practitioner pattern. The fair-lending committee, conduct committee, or consumer-protection committee is generally the senior-management body.

#### Evidence patterns

Conduct acceptance criteria emphasise customer-facing evidence: redress disbursement reconciliation, consumer communication delivery confirmation, complaint-trending dashboards, mystery-shopping results, fair-lending statistical-analysis output. Counts of customers affected, dollars disbursed, and complaints received are the operative metrics.

#### Plan posture

Conduct plans triggered by a public consent order generally carry `medium` confidence at most pre-closure; the monitor or independent consultant's confirmation is the gating step for closure attestation, not the firm's internal sign-off.

#### Sources

Named regulatory anchors for conduct implementation are in `references/source-anchors.md`. Citations include the CFPB Supervision and Examination Manual, CFPB UDAAP examination procedures, ECOA and Regulation B, the Fair Housing Act, Regulation C (HMDA), the OCC and FDIC fair-lending examination procedures, FTC Section 5, and published consent orders for redress and remediation pattern.

## cyber

### Cross-cutting overlay — cyber

Loaded when the scope flags cyber. Implementation plans triggered by or touching cyber rules carry distinct timing, governance, and evidence patterns.

#### Incident-notification regimes the plan must reconcile

A cyber-touching plan generally lands inside multiple notification regimes. Each has a clock. The plan reconciles them rather than picking one.

- **12 CFR Part 53 (banking 36-hour rule)** — banking organisations notify the primary federal regulator within 36 hours of determining that a notification incident has occurred. §53.3 sets the trigger; §53.2 defines covered entities.
- **Bank service provider notification** under 12 CFR Part 53 §53.4 — bank service providers notify each affected bank as soon as possible.
- **NYDFS 23 NYCRR Part 500 §500.17** — covered entities notify NYDFS within 72 hours of determining a cybersecurity event has occurred. §500.17(a) sets the standard.
- **SEC 8-K Item 1.05** — public companies disclose material cybersecurity incidents within four business days of materiality determination.
- **State breach-notification laws** — every US state has one; clocks and triggers vary by state.
- **FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314 §314.5)** — financial institutions under FTC jurisdiction notify FTC as soon as possible and no later than 30 days after discovery of an event affecting 500+ consumers.
- **HIPAA Breach Notification Rule** — for covered entities and business associates handling PHI.
- **EU GDPR Article 33** — supervisory-authority notification within 72 hours where applicable.
- **EU DORA Article 19** — major ICT-related incident reporting where applicable.

The plan's notification workstream names every applicable regime, the fastest clock, and the workflow that triggers each notification from a single materiality assessment.

#### Governance pattern

Cyber-touching implementation plans run with the CISO as a workstream owner and the disclosure committee (or its equivalent) as a governance checkpoint with out-of-cycle escalation. The disclosure committee's standing cyber agenda item is the practitioner pattern; quarterly cadence is too slow for a four-business-day clock.

Board-level cyber oversight is increasingly explicit under SEC Regulation S-K Item 106(c). The plan's board-reporting cadence reflects the rule's expectation that the board has visibility into cyber risk management.

#### Control-design patterns

For NYDFS Part 500 implementations, milestones map to §500.2 (programme), §500.3 (policy), §500.4 (CISO), §500.5 (testing), §500.6 (audit trail), §500.7 (access privileges), §500.9 (risk assessment), §500.11 (third-party SP security), §500.13 (asset inventory), §500.14 (training and monitoring), §500.15 (encryption), §500.16 (incident response), §500.17 (notification), §500.21 (annual certification or written acknowledgment).

For FFIEC IT Examination Handbook implementations, the booklet structure (Information Security; Outsourcing Technology Services; Business Continuity; Architecture, Infrastructure, and Operations; Audit; Development and Acquisition; Management) is the workstream taxonomy.

#### Evidence patterns

Cyber acceptance criteria emphasise testable evidence: pen-test reports, control-test workpapers, access-recertification logs, incident-response tabletop after-action records, training-completion attestations. Activity language ("implement MFA") fails examiners; evidence language ("MFA enforced for all privileged-access users by [date]; coverage validated by quarterly access-recertification report") passes.

For Part 500 §500.21 annual certification, the implementation plan integrates with the firm's annual cyber attestation cycle; the obligation lands in BAU as a recurring annual milestone.

#### Plan posture

Cyber-touching plans elevate confidence to `medium` at most for a reactive remediation; `high` confidence requires that the control has run through at least one full audit or test cycle and the testable evidence is in hand. Pre-go-live confidence is generally `medium` regardless of build quality.

#### Sources

Named regulatory anchors for cyber implementation are in `references/source-anchors.md`. Citations include 12 CFR Part 53, NYDFS 23 NYCRR Part 500, FFIEC IT Examination Handbook, SEC Reg S-K Item 106 and Form 8-K Item 1.05, FTC Safeguards Rule, EU DORA where applicable.

## privacy

### Cross-cutting overlay — privacy

Loaded when the scope flags privacy. Implementation plans triggered by or touching privacy rules carry distinct data-mapping, consent, and consumer-rights patterns.

#### GLBA Safeguards Rule remediation

For financial institutions under FTC jurisdiction, the FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314, as amended December 2021 with subsequent amendments) drives the information-security programme requirements. Sections relevant to implementation plans include:

- §314.4(a) qualified individual designation;
- §314.4(b) risk assessment;
- §314.4(c) safeguards (access controls, encryption, MFA, logging, training, third-party oversight);
- §314.4(d) testing and monitoring;
- §314.4(e) training;
- §314.4(f) service-provider oversight;
- §314.4(h) incident-response plan;
- §314.4(i) annual report to the board or governing body;
- §314.5 notification (effective 2024).

For banks under Regulation P (12 CFR Part 1016) and the interagency Information Security Guidelines (12 CFR Part 30 Appendix B for OCC; FRB equivalents), the framework is parallel. The plan names the rule the entity falls under.

#### Consumer-privacy overlay (state and federal)

Implementation plans touching state consumer-privacy laws (CCPA/CPRA, VCDPA, CPA, CTDPA, UCPA, IDPA, OCPA, MCDPA, TIPA, MTCDPA, others as adopted) integrate with the firm's consumer-privacy programme. Common workstream elements:

- data-mapping for in-scope personal information categories;
- consumer-rights workflow (right to know, right to delete, right to correct, right to opt out of sale or sharing, right to limit sensitive personal information, right to opt out of profiling);
- consumer-facing privacy notice updates;
- vendor and service-provider contractual updates (Data Processing Agreements);
- training and recordkeeping;
- annual reporting where required (CPRA cybersecurity audit and risk assessment under California regulation).

Federal-level consumer-financial-data rules (CFPB Section 1033 Personal Financial Data Rights rule, October 2024 final) bring an obligation set adjacent to but distinct from state privacy laws; the plan names which obligations land where.

#### EU GDPR overlay where applicable

For EU-touching firms, GDPR Articles 5-22 (principles, lawful basis, data-subject rights), Articles 24-43 (controller and processor obligations), Articles 33-34 (notification), and Articles 44-50 (international transfers) frame the obligations. Schrems II and the EU-US Data Privacy Framework drive transfer mechanism choices. The plan integrates with the firm's GDPR programme rather than running parallel.

#### Workstream and milestone shape

Typical privacy workstream pattern:

- data-mapping update reflecting the new obligation set;
- system changes for consumer-rights workflow (intake, identity verification, fulfilment, recordkeeping);
- privacy notice and disclosure updates;
- vendor / processor contract amendment cycle;
- training across the in-scope first-line population;
- testing and monitoring against the new requirement;
- annual reporting build (where the rule requires).

#### Evidence patterns

Privacy acceptance criteria emphasise data-subject-rights fulfilment SLAs (e.g., CCPA 45-day right-to-know window with 45-day extension), consent records, opt-out signal handling logs, and vendor-contract amendment evidence. Activity language fails ("update privacy notice"); evidence language passes ("privacy notice v8.0 deployed across in-scope channels with sample-test of presentation rate").

Citations include FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314), Regulation P (12 CFR Part 1016), CFPB Section 1033 final rule (12 CFR Part 1033), CCPA/CPRA (Cal. Civ. Code §1798 et seq.), state privacy laws as adopted, EU GDPR (Regulation 2016/679).
